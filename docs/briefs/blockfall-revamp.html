Blockfall · Roblox revamp · design plan · lobby revision · 24 Sep 2026

From rectangles to real pixel art

Today every body, weapon and effect in the Roblox build is drawn out of pooled coloured rectangles, with no image assets at all. That kept the port honest, but it is the ceiling on how good the game can look. This plan swaps those rectangles for hand-drawn sprite sheets. On top of that it redesigns the look of all 12 weapons, adds 2 new ones, gives every ultimate a proper cutscene moment, rebuilds the 6 maps, adds 3 new maps, adds a natural-disaster system, and completely rebuilds the shared walkable lobby. The engine, the netcode and the balance work you've already done all stay.

Ground rules

This plan covers art, content and new systems. The foundations underneath stay put, because they're what makes the game fair and stable. Every idea below had to follow these five rules.

1. The web version stays the source of truth.

Any change to gameplay (weapon moves, map layouts, disasters) goes into web/src first. From there it's generated into Luau and checked by check.sh. The browser game stays playable at every step, and a change that only lands in Roblox counts as a bug.

2. Sprites come from the game state, never from a clock.

Which sprite frame is shown depends only on the fighter's state, move and move-frame. Nothing is time-based. That's what keeps rollback prediction from making sprites stutter, and it lets the web and Roblox builds be tested to show the exact same frame.

3. What you see is where you can be hit.

A fighter's body sprite may only stick out past its hurtbox by a small, measured amount. The sprite tool checks every frame and fails the build if one breaks this. Weapons live on their own layer, and hitboxes have their own debug overlay.

4. Disasters are hazards, never coin flips.

Every disaster gives at least 1.5 seconds of warning. Each has a safe place to stand and a timing-based way to dodge it. Its damage runs through the normal knockback formula, so it can never KO a low-percent fighter outright. Disasters are always off in ranked.

5. Sprites can be added a bit at a time.

Any move that doesn't have sprites yet falls back to today's rectangle renderer. That means art can arrive one weapon at a time and the game never sits half-broken while it's being drawn.

What stays exactly as it is

  • The 60 Hz game engine, knockback, the two-curve hitstun system and the balance pass (ultimates now KO at 132–213%)
  • Server authority, input prediction and rollback, and the snapshot and scene streams
  • All 12 weapons' frame data. Changes below are flagged per weapon and go through a balance test first
  • The map layouts of Foundry Floor and The Span (the fairest competitive maps don't get redesigned)
  • The dark, gritty pixel-art direction from the September 21 art pass

What gets replaced or added

  • How things are drawn: sprite sheets on reused image slots instead of stacks of rectangles. That's about 9 image slots per fighter, down from about 54 rectangles today
  • Animation: hand-drawn key poses timed exactly to each move's frame data
  • Weapons: new designs, visual effects for every ability, and 2 new weapons
  • Ultimates: a shared cutscene moment (pause, zoom, character banner), then a unique effect for each weapon
  • Lobby: a rebuilt Iron Concourse, three physical mode gates, and walkable preparation rooms with map boards, illustrated weapon racks and explicit ready stations
  • Maps: layered moving backgrounds on all 9, plus 3 new layouts
  • Natural disasters: a new engine system with 6 disasters and 3 modes (Off, Stage, Chaos)

Art direction

Dark, gritty pixel art at 8 pixels per stud

The overall look stays the dark industrial style from the September 21 pass: dark silhouettes, warm furnace light, cool rim light. What changes is the drawing scale. Every sprite is drawn at 8 pixels per stud, and that number comes straight from the camera.

How characters get recoloured

Players can recolour their avatars, so body sprites can't have fixed colours. Instead, each body part is drawn as a greyscale layer (outline, primary, secondary, tertiary). In Roblox each layer is tinted with ImageColor3; on the web it goes through the existing tint cache in pixels2d.js. The greyscale shading carries through the tint, so one sprite sheet works for every avatar and colour. Heads, faces and hats are separate small sprites that snap onto a head anchor point exported from each frame.

Readability rules for every sprite

  • Silhouette first. Each weapon's outline must be recognisable as a solid black shape at 50% zoom.
  • Three brightness levels. Dark background, mid-tone fighters, bright hit frames. The frames where an attack can actually hit are always the brightest thing on screen.
  • Dodge frames get an outline. While a fighter can't be hit (spot dodge frames 3–14, air dodge 3–13), a 1-pixel pale outline shows it.
  • Warnings are cool, damage is hot. Every warning uses cyan (#7FD4FF) and every damage zone uses orange (#F08B60), across all 6 disasters and all 14 ultimates.
  • No blood. Roblox content rules count realistic blood toward the game's maturity rating. The Daggers "bleed" and anything similar use crimson crystal shards and sparks instead.

How sprites get drawn on screen

SpriteCanvas.luau sits next to Canvas2D and keeps the same reuse-and-hide pooling. The difference is it reuses ImageLabels with ImageRectOffset/ImageRectSize and ResampleMode = Pixelated. It uses the same camera math as Canvas2D.world, so sprites and any remaining rectangle-drawn parts line up exactly. Only the sprite sheets needed for the current match (its fighters' weapons plus its map) get preloaded, which is about 8 images.

Animation

Hand-drawn poses, timed to the frame data

channels.js already builds in five animation principles: anticipation, slow-in/slow-out, follow-through, overlapping action and moving holds. The revamp keeps that timing and replaces the maths-generated poses with hand-drawn key poses that hold. Pixel-art fighting games don't smoothly blend between poses; they hold a drawing and then snap to the next one. Every move in Aseprite is a tag. Each frame's duration is a whole number of game frames, and the total has to exactly match startup + active + endlag. If it doesn't, gen-sprites.mjs fails the build.

Shared body animations

These are drawn once and shared by every weapon, then tinted for each avatar. State-driven animations like rising, falling and tumbling pick their frame from the fighter's velocity or spin angle, not from a timer. That keeps them correct after a rollback.

AnimationGame framesKey posesChosen byNotes

How each move's poses are laid out

  • Anticipation (first ~40% of startup): the body pulls back and goes past the pose slightly. This is the frame the defender reacts to, so it can't be shortened for style.
  • Last startup frame: either the fully wound-up pose, or a smear drawing (a stretched streak) for moves faster than 8 frames.
  • Active frames: the contact pose with the brightest colours. Swing trails are drawn on the weapon layer, and the weapon swing path comes from WeaponPose so it matches the hitbox.
  • Recovery: follow-through that overshoots, then a settle with a wobble. Heavy weapons (Axe, Hammer) get more recovery poses so they read as heavier.

Weapons & abilities

Fourteen weapons, forged from the foundry

Revised in v2. The first version was a set of loose fantasy props. These share one design language that fits the world the maps are set in: salvage-forged, made from rivet guns, blast doors, wrecking balls and forge sledges. Each one also puts its special mechanic on the weapon, so you don't have to look at the HUD for it.

These pictures are the game's own art, not mockups. Every weapon is a pixel grid in web/src/data/weaponArt.js at 8 px per stud. Both the web build and Roblox now draw it, and it's live on branch revamp/sprites-disasters. Each weapon reaches within one pixel of its hitboxes (a test checks this). The pale panel is the silhouette test: every weapon has to be recognisable as a flat dark shape. IDs, frame data and balance don't change.

Ultimates

One shared cutscene, fourteen payoffs

Every ultimate follows the same opening, using timings that already exist: ULTIMATE.freezeFrames = 26, invincibleFrames = 22. For the first 26 frames the action pauses while the scene dims to 35%. The camera zooms in 1.35× on the user over 8 frames, and a diagonal banner with their portrait, weapon and ultimate name sweeps across (6 frames in, 12 held, 6 out). During the 22 invincible frames the user has a gold outline, so opponents can see it. After that each weapon plays its own effect, and the impact adds hit-pause, a 2-frame white flash and a big screen shake. Each storyboard below is a to-scale pixel mock of the three key moments.

New scope · complete lobby rebuild · planned, not implemented

The Iron Concourse: a place to play in

An inhabited, salvage-built fight terminal above a working foundry. Players arrive as their own pixel fighters, run across worn steel decking, meet other players, and enter one of three enormous industrial gates. Beyond each gate is a walkable preparation room. The world is the lobby; menus are small tools attached to objects inside it.

Client requirements stay intact.

Exactly three play entrances: 1v1, 2v2 and 3v3. No map doors, no free-for-all entrance, no full-screen mode-select HUD on spawn. Map, weapon, avatar, rules and bot choices appear only after a mode has been entered. Bots are OFF by default; empty slots wait for people.

This revision extends the supplied Blockfall Revamp and its Animation Bible. It supersedes the old lobby visual/layout brief, not the existing room-authority rules. The current main project already has a shared concourse and room service; the revamp worktree does not yet contain that whole implementation. Integrate it deliberately before building these views. The source attachment and generated animation bible remain unchanged.

1. Art direction: the same foundry, not a different game

Materials and silhouette

Blackened iron, riveted salvage plates, chipped enamel signage, patched cables, brick furnace housings and worn brass. Large readable silhouettes first; clustered pixel wear at corners and contact points, not random noise everywhere. Gates borrow the weapons' blast-door, rivet-gun and forge-sledge construction language.

Light and colour

Use the plan's soot #0B1017, steel #18212D, muted metal #33404F, amber #E5AB65 and bone lettering #E9E5DB. Warm furnace light below and behind grates; subdued cool rim light from roof windows. Mid-tone fighters and readable signs remain above background detail.

Richness through depth

Four scene layers: distant smoke stacks and freight silhouettes; middle-distance furnace and crane machinery; the crisp playable deck and stations; sparse foreground chains and rail ends. Proposed parallax ratios are 0.08 / 0.25 / 1.0 / 1.05. Nothing crosses a fighter's face, a gate label or an interaction target.

Pixel and signal discipline

Draw world assets at 8 px/stud, with nearest-neighbour sampling and shared camera alignment. No smooth gradients, vector-looking glossy panels or upscaled blurry paintings. Cyan warning pulses and hot damage shapes remain combat signals; lobby interaction uses steady amber brackets, words and icons. Decorative heat stays behind barriers, never on the walkable floor.

One low industrial music bed, quiet furnace rumble, soft metal footsteps and a distinct gate latch/ready chime. No sirens or combat hit sounds for normal navigation. Limit overlapping machine sounds; distant ambience ducks while reading a board. Reduced-motion mode freezes ambient flipbooks and parallax without hiding room status.

2. Spatial layout: three landmarks on one clear route

Keep the current fast, flat travel spine as the blockout: floor x = −64…94, spawn x = 0, gates at −28 / 14 / 56. Rebuild the architecture, props, backgrounds, camera framing and rooms around it. Every required destination is reachable without jumping. Optional catwalks, seating ledges and crate steps are social/play space, never the only route to a match.

3. Choose a mode first, then prepare inside its room

  1. Approach and enter. A nearby gate gains an amber edge and a readable interaction prompt. Merely walking past cannot queue the player. Use the configured interact binding (currently E / controller X) or a touch action. Display bindings from the input source, not hard-coded tutorial art.
  2. Find a room in that mode. The threshold's small room board lists same-server rooms and free human places, with Join / Create. Auto-select only when the choice is unambiguous and the player confirms it. A full room stays visibly full; never put someone in another mode or replace them with a CPU.
  3. Walk into the preparation bay. The player and that room's members move around a real side-view space. A clear exit returns to the concourse. One shared room template changes width and team markers for 2, 4 or 6 fighters; it is not three independent systems.
  4. Map board. The host interacts with a wall-mounted stage display. It shows large stage images, name, geometry thumbnail and rules/hazard labels. Show released, playable stages only: six now, expanding toward the plan's nine as R6 lands. Members can inspect the selected map but cannot overwrite host settings. Ranked/disaster options appear only when their systems exist.
  5. Weapon racks and lockers. The racks visibly display the real weapons before interaction. A focused catalogue then shows actual art, name, archetype and selected state. Next to it, the player's full fighter preview uses their actual avatar colours and equipped weapon. Reuse weaponArt.js/the sprite manifest as it lands; use the current renderer as fallback. Ship the existing 12; Rapier and Chakram become selectable only when R7 is playable. Lockers edit only the local player's appearance.
  6. Rules console. Host-only stocks, items and explicit per-empty-slot bot controls. The default sign reads BOTS OFF — WAITING FOR PLAYERS. Adding a CPU requires a deliberate action and shows CPU plus difficulty beside that slot; no timer-based autofill. Other members see the rules read-only. Reset bots off on a new room and after a match.
  7. Ready rail. Each player confirms readiness at their team's physical station. A stamped check, player name and lit marker show the accepted state. Standing on the platform is not consent; movement does not toggle readiness. Empty, CPU, ready and not-ready places use words/shapes, not colour alone. Host starts only with complete equal teams and every human ready. Roster, map, rules or loadout changes invalidate readiness.
  8. Launch and return. Preload the chosen map and fighter art while the room remains visible. The server validates the room revision and acknowledges the launch before any shutter covers the scene. Keep a loading label and Cancel/Back path; on asset or server failure restore the intact bay with a useful error. After the result, return to that room's preparation bay for a rematch, with ready flags cleared and bots off; its exit returns to the concourse.

Panels open only at the object being used and close back to the same walkable space. They must not become one full-screen setup dashboard. On phones a focused sheet may temporarily occupy the screen for legibility; it closes back to the same station and is never the default lobby. Keyboard, controller and touch can complete the whole route. No combat controls leak through while chatting, editing an avatar or using a board.

4. Animation brief: reuse the bible, add life to the building

Use the Animation Bible's actual production cells: 112 × 96 px underlays, feet anchored at (44, 80), approximately 42 px body height, and separate outline/primary/secondary/tertiary tint masks plus weapon/feature layers. Use this working-cell layout for the lobby, superseding the original plan's 48 × 48 cell suggestion: a 1024-square sheet fits at most 9 × 10 of these cells before padding, not 441. Reuse shared idle, walk, run, jumpsquat, air and landing poses without redrawing a separate lobby character.

Keep the bible's hold, don't tween rule: idle is a 60-frame loop, walk a 32-frame distance-driven cycle, run a 24-frame distance-driven cycle; rise/apex/fall comes from velocity and landing from state frames. Do not add combat, invincibility, new movement speeds or animation locks to make the lobby feel lively. Walk art is available where analog movement uses it; do not silently add an unsupported movement mechanic.

  • Art package: four parallax layers; one modular deck/wall/trim/pipe tileset; three gate skins; one preparation-room kit with 2/4/6-slot variants; map-board, rack, locker, console and ready-rail props; gate numerals and status icon set; the 62 drawings above. Stage thumbnails and weapon images reuse their source art. No new NPC cast, store, currency or bespoke weapon-preview animation is required.
  • Estimate: allow an additional 60–90 artist-hours for the lobby environment kit, layers, new drawings, export and one revision pass. This is a planning allowance, not a quote or a measured throughput claim; re-estimate after one finished gate and room sample. Engineering and live QA are separate. Do not quietly absorb this work into the original 350–400-hour estimate.
  • Shared source: extend web/src/data/hub.js with scene, collision and station definitions. Keep room-scene data separate from match stages. Generate the Luau data; never hand-edit roblox/src/shared/Data. Port shared movement/interaction behavior together and retain replay parity.
  • World renderer: evolve Hub2D.luau and the web lobby presentation onto the R0 sprite path. Reuse body/weapon atlases, palette masks and camera math. Draw distant rooms only when visible; pool and hide sprites, not create/destroy them each frame.
  • Room views: split the current RoomPanel responsibilities across station-focused views in the walkable bay, preserving its validated commands. Natural disasters

A new engine system, not a visual effect

Disasters live in a new file, web/src/engine/disasters.js, ported to roblox/src/shared/Disasters.luau and checked by a disaster-parity replay test. They use the stage's seeded random generator, so a match can still be replayed exactly from its seed and inputs. They're separate from the 10 existing hazards. Hazards are small, always-on features of a map. Disasters are big, scheduled events with warnings.

When they happen

The first one comes at 45 s at the earliest, then every 40–55 s (the seed decides exactly when). None during the countdown or sudden death.

Warning

At least 90 frames of warning, with a cyan warning shape in the world plus an edge-of-screen arrow on the HUD if it's off camera.

Damage

Goes through launchSpeed/stunSpeed with low knockback growth. The rule is no KO below 140%, and a test enforces it.

Two ways to survive

A place to stand that's safe, and a timing-based dodge. A disaster that can only be escaped by running away is a chore, not a skill.

Never unfair

Doesn't affect respawn platforms or invincible fighters. Never targets just one player.

Modes

Off (ranked, always) · Stage (each map's own disaster, default for casual) · Chaos (the 4 disasters that work on any map rotate on every casual map).

Testing: in the bot soak test (144 matches), disasters must cause ≤ 15% of all KOs. For each disaster, a scripted fighter standing in the listed safe zone takes 0 damage across 100 seeded occurrences. As with every other test in this repo, each of these gets checked by deliberately breaking the code and confirming the test catches it.

Order of work, and how each step is proven done

Lobby addition: L0–L3 below are new milestones interleaved with the original roadmap. They are planned, not implemented. Add the separately scoped lobby asset workload; the original art estimate does not include it.

The code phases are fairly small. Drawing the art is the slow part. A rough estimate: about 1,500 finished, layered key poses at around 6 an hour, plus 30-odd background layers. That comes to roughly 350–400 hours of art, which is why rule 5 exists: the game can ship with some moves still drawn the old way.
